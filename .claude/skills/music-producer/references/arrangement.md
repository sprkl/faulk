# Arrangement and finishing

How material becomes a track: choosing a form, laying it on bars, blurring the seams, placing surprises, and knowing when to stop. Strategies are summarised in [creative-strategies.md](creative-strategies.md); this file turns them into bar numbers.

## Two models of form

**Form by sections** (pop, songs with vocals, hip-hop): verse, chorus, bridge, pre-chorus, intro, outro, each with its own material. Typical lengths at 4/4: intro 4–8, verse 8–16, pre-chorus 4–8, chorus 8–16, bridge 8, outro 4–8. Common shapes: `Intro A B A B C B (B)`; hip-hop `Intro V16 H8 V16 H8 V16 H8 Outro` where the hook is 4 or 8 bars. A pop track runs 3 to 4 minutes, so about 90–110 bars at 120 bpm.

**Form by density** (instrumental electronic music, club tracks, drum and bass): the same core material throughout, sections defined by how many layers play. Typical shape for a club track at 124 bpm, about 5½ minutes: intro 16–32 (drums thin, one element), build 16, groove 32 (full except the lead), break 16 (drums out, harmony exposed), peak 32 (everything), breakdown 16, second peak 32, outro 16–32 (mirror of the intro, mixable). Multiples of 8 and 16 everywhere so that the next section always starts where a DJ expects it.

Choose one model per track, or state the hybrid (a song with a club-style breakdown). A track without vocals rarely needs a verse and a chorus; a song rarely needs a 32-bar intro.

Density has more than one shape. The layered one above (an intro, one part entering at a time, a break, a peak, an exit by subtraction) is a habit, not a design: [forms.md](forms.md) lists it as F0 next to eleven others (reverse arc, silence-hinged drop, single long crescendo, theme and variations, two tracks in one, loop with one interruption, call and response, A B A' with another meter, false ending, rondo, through-composed morph), each with a bar map, and gives the rule that picks one so that two runs in a row never share a shape.

## The dramatic arc on bars

Map the arc onto the chosen form: exposition = intro and first section (materials introduced one at a time); rising action = variations and added layers; climax = the most intense moment, placed where the form puts it (at the start in a reverse arc, at 85–95 % in a single crescendo, around 55–70 % in a ladder); falling action = whatever the form asks, not necessarily a mirror of the rise (parts can leave in a different order from their entry, or the track can cut); dénouement = restatement or dissolution. Intensity is density, register, rhythmic subdivision, harmony or silence, not only the number of parts. For static genres, replace the climax with a slow, continuous change (a filter opening over 64 bars, a noise bed thickening) and let the form breathe through subtraction only.

Give the arc a harmonic path as well as a density path ([theory.md](theory.md) → Progressions): the climax sounds in another chord picture, key or mode than the exposition, not only louder. A plan for 96 bars: intro 1–16 chord-less or on one chord; A 17–32 cycle 1; B 33–48 cycle 2, sharing one chord with cycle 1; break 49–56 a pedal or a held sus chord; peak 57–80 on the relative key or lifted (up a whole step or a minor third at bar 65); exit 81–96 cycle 1 returns while parts leave. A cycle or key change lands on a section boundary, never mid-phrase.

## Subtractive workflow

1. Decide the total length in bars and the tempo.
2. Fill every track with its pattern for the whole length. Make no choices; take under a minute.
3. Carve: mute parts per section until the densest section is right, then work backwards to the start removing more at each step, then forward to the end. Subtraction suits the density forms; for a form built on transformation (F3 crescendo, F4 variations, F11 morph), carve by parameter instead: list what changes in each section (register, subdivision, key, filter) and keep the set of parts mostly constant.
4. Lengthen or shorten sections by inserting or deleting whole bars (keep multiples of 4 unless the irregularity is the idea).
5. Write the result as the arrangement block of the production sheet: every section, every part that plays in it.

In the sheet, a part whose notes differ in two sections is two patterns (`Beat A`, `Beat A fill`); a part that enters late, leaves early or skips a bar is one pattern with a window (`Beat A [from=65.2]`, `Lead [to=95.3]`, `Bass A [mute=27]`); a part that is merely filtered or quieter stays one pattern and the processing goes in prose. Sections stay 8 or 16 bars long: the windows carry the seam edits, so there is no need to cut the form into 4-bar slices to express them.

## Blurring the seams

For each section change, pick at least one coarse edit and, at the important changes, one fine edit:

- **Coarse**: one or two parts carry over the line for 1–4 bars; one part stops 1–2 bars early; one part is cut on either side of the line so silence marks the change. Never all parts at once.
- **Anticipation**: a fill or riser that peaks a sixteenth before the downbeat, silence on beat 1, re-entry on beat 2 with a crash or the full chord.
- **Hesitation**: a fill that continues over the line and ends on beat 2 of the new section while everything else has already changed; the new drums simply take over.
- **Harmony**: the bass or the chord anticipates the new section by an eighth or a beat, or holds past it.

A boundary that arrives by itself, with every track changing on beat 1, is the sound of the grid. Allow it only where a hard cut is the intended effect, and then make it total: silence, or a single hit. A silence-hinged drop (F2) is such a case: one empty bar before the new section is the whole seam; add no riser to it.

## Unique events

Place three to six things that happen once in the track: a one-shot sample at a formally insignificant moment, off the grid; a one-time variation inside a phrase (a transformed copy of a pattern, used for one bar); an effect switched on for two beats and off again; a note of an instrument heard nowhere else. Mark each one in the sheet with its bar and beat. Two in a row teach the listener to expect them; spread them.

At least one of the three to six is **structural**: a change the listener hears as form rather than as a sound. Each counts as one event. Ideas:

- one bar of silence, then the loop returns over a different bass note, everything else untouched;
- a tape stop on the whole mix for one beat, then a clean restart at tempo;
- an eight-bar phrase replayed at once on another instrument, without drums;
- the drums drop out for two bars while the lead plays straight eighths that keep the pulse, and the drums return off the downbeat;
- a role swap: the bass plays the melody for four bars while the lead holds the root;
- one bar of 3/4 or 5/4 inside a 4/4 section (name the irregular bar);
- a two-bar phrase at half speed, or reversed just before its return;
- the last chord of a section frozen and held as a drone under the first four bars of the next;
- one out-of-key note held for a beat (named as a decision), answered by the next phrase two bars later;
- everything mono for two bars, then wide again.

## Endings

Three honest endings: the **restatement** (a final section that returns to the first material, often the chorus or the groove, and stops clean after a unique event); the **dissolution** (layers removed one by one over 16–32 bars until a single element remains, then silence or a long tail); the **cut** (a hard stop on beat 1, possibly with a reverb throw). Two more, for the forms built for them: the **false end** (a dissolution to a tail and a bar or two of silence, then a restart in a new feel or key: F9), and the **unresolved end** (a stop on a note or chord that wants to resolve, left hanging). A fade-out is a dissolution done by the fader; prefer doing it with the arrangement.

## Good enough

The track is finished when the next change would be arbitrary. Check once, concretely, then stop:

- every section has a reason to be its length and the boundaries are blurred where they should be;
- the most intense moment is the best of the track and is placed where the form puts it (a rondo or a theme and variations may return to it by design);
- the form is not one of the two previous runs' forms ([forms.md](forms.md) → Choosing the form);
- at least two sections have a different chord picture, or the single cycle is the stated idea;
- there are unique events, at least one of them structural, and not too many;
- the bass and the chords agree, or disagree on purpose;
- the melody, if any, has its peak once and its phrases answer each other;
- the last 10 % of edits made the track better, not different. If they only made it different, revert them and declare it done.

Write down what a second version could explore, and move to the next track.
