# Arrangement and finishing

How material becomes a track: choosing a form, laying it on bars, blurring the seams, placing surprises, and knowing when to stop. Strategies are summarised in [creative-strategies.md](creative-strategies.md); this file turns them into bar numbers.

## Two models of form

**Form by sections** (pop, songs with vocals, hip-hop): verse, chorus, bridge, pre-chorus, intro, outro, each with its own material. Typical lengths at 4/4: intro 4–8, verse 8–16, pre-chorus 4–8, chorus 8–16, bridge 8, outro 4–8. Common shapes: `Intro A B A B C B (B)`; hip-hop `Intro V16 H8 V16 H8 V16 H8 Outro` where the hook is 4 or 8 bars. A pop track runs 3 to 4 minutes, so about 90–110 bars at 120 bpm.

**Form by density** (instrumental electronic music, club tracks, drum and bass): the same core material throughout, sections defined by how many layers play. Typical shape for a club track at 124 bpm, about 5½ minutes: intro 16–32 (drums thin, one element), build 16, groove 32 (full except the lead), break 16 (drums out, harmony exposed), peak 32 (everything), breakdown 16, second peak 32, outro 16–32 (mirror of the intro, mixable). Multiples of 8 and 16 everywhere so that the next section always starts where a DJ expects it.

Choose one model per track, or state the hybrid (a song with a club-style breakdown). A track without vocals rarely needs a verse and a chorus; a song rarely needs a 32-bar intro.

## The dramatic arc on bars

Map the arc onto the chosen form: exposition = intro and first section (materials introduced one at a time); rising action = variations and added layers; climax = the densest section, placed around 55–70 % of the way through; falling action = a shorter mirror of the rise; dénouement = restatement or dissolution. For static genres, replace the climax with a slow, continuous change (a filter opening over 64 bars, a noise bed thickening) and let the form breathe through subtraction only.

## Subtractive workflow

1. Decide the total length in bars and the tempo.
2. Fill every track with its pattern for the whole length. Make no choices; take under a minute.
3. Carve: mute parts per section until the densest section is right, then work backwards to the start removing more at each step, then forward to the end.
4. Lengthen or shorten sections by inserting or deleting whole bars (keep multiples of 4 unless the irregularity is the idea).
5. Write the result as the arrangement block of the production sheet: every section, every part that plays in it.

In the sheet, a part whose notes differ in two sections is two patterns (`Beat A`, `Beat A fill`); a part that enters late, leaves early or skips a bar is one pattern with a window (`Beat A [from=65.2]`, `Lead [to=95.3]`, `Bass A [mute=27]`); a part that is merely filtered or quieter stays one pattern and the processing goes in prose. Sections stay 8 or 16 bars long: the windows carry the seam edits, so there is no need to cut the form into 4-bar slices to express them.

## Blurring the seams

For each section change, pick at least one coarse edit and, at the important changes, one fine edit:

- **Coarse**: one or two parts carry over the line for 1–4 bars; one part stops 1–2 bars early; one part is cut on either side of the line so silence marks the change. Never all parts at once.
- **Anticipation**: a fill or riser that peaks a sixteenth before the downbeat, silence on beat 1, re-entry on beat 2 with a crash or the full chord.
- **Hesitation**: a fill that continues over the line and ends on beat 2 of the new section while everything else has already changed; the new drums simply take over.
- **Harmony**: the bass or the chord anticipates the new section by an eighth or a beat, or holds past it.

A boundary that arrives by itself, with every track changing on beat 1, is the sound of the grid. Allow it only where a hard cut is the intended effect, and then make it total: silence, or a single hit.

## Unique events

Place three to six things that happen once in the track: a one-shot sample at a formally insignificant moment, off the grid; a one-time variation inside a phrase (a transformed copy of a pattern, used for one bar); an effect switched on for two beats and off again; a note of an instrument heard nowhere else. Mark each one in the sheet with its bar and beat. Two in a row teach the listener to expect them; spread them.

## Endings

Three honest endings: the **restatement** (a final section that returns to the first material, often the chorus or the groove, and stops clean after a unique event); the **dissolution** (layers removed one by one over 16–32 bars until a single element remains, then silence or a long tail); the **cut** (a hard stop on beat 1, possibly with a reverb throw). A fade-out is a dissolution done by the fader; prefer doing it with the arrangement.

## Good enough

The track is finished when the next change would be arbitrary. Check once, concretely, then stop:

- every section has a reason to be its length and the boundaries are blurred where they should be;
- the densest section is the best moment of the track and arrives once;
- there are unique events, and not too many;
- the bass and the chords agree, or disagree on purpose;
- the melody, if any, has its peak once and its phrases answer each other;
- the last 10 % of edits made the track better, not different. If they only made it different, revert them and declare it done.

Write down what a second version could explore, and move to the next track.
