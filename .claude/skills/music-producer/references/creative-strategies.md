# Creative strategies: problem → move

Each entry names a symptom you can recognise in the brief, the project or your own draft, then the move that answers it. The five parameters of music used throughout are **sound** (timbre, texture, space), **harmony**, **melody**, **rhythm** and **form**. Read the part that matches where the work is stuck: beginning (nothing exists yet), progressing (material exists, no direction), finishing (a draft exists, it will not close).

## Beginning

### Catalog of attributes
**Symptom.** A reference track inspires the work, and the risk is copying it or ignoring it.
**Move.** Describe the reference, then put it away and build from the description only. Write one concrete line per parameter: tempo; sound elements and their roles; harmony (key, chord cycle, modulations); melody (range, contour, where it lives); rhythm (pulse, where the accents fall, loop lengths); form (section lengths, additive or subtractive layering, where the break is). The catalog is a scaffold, not a transcription: two producers reading it would make two different tracks. When the brief names references, produce this catalog as the first deliverable and never consult the reference again.

### Active listening
**Symptom.** You know what a style sounds like but cannot say what makes it work.
**Move.** Listen (or, for an agent, analyse) in passes, one parameter per pass: sound, harmony, melody, rhythm, form. Then in chunks: one or two bars looped until every event is accounted for, then widen. Then subjectively: name the moment that produces the emotional response and the mechanism behind it. Each finding becomes a line of the catalog or a candidate move.

### Arbitrary constraints
**Symptom.** Every option is valid, so nothing gets chosen.
**Move.** Remove valid options on purpose, before starting, and work inside the frame. Musical constraints: every sound from one sample; drop an instrument the genre expects (a dance track with no hi-hats, a song with no bass) and either fill the hole another way or leave the space open; one key, one chord, one bar of rhythm for the whole track. Working constraints: a deadline, a fixed number of tracks, a fixed set of instruments. Because the constraints are arbitrary, dropping one later costs nothing; choosing none costs the start. Pick two or three, write them down, keep them until they hurt.

### Presets as starting points
**Symptom.** Sound design eats the session before a note is written, or using presets feels like cheating.
**Move.** Pick a preset in the right family (a bass preset for a bass part), write the notes and rhythm, then come back and bend the sound until it is yours: filter cutoff, resonance, and the four envelope stages already cover most of the distance. Own the sound by the end, not at the start.

### Simple sounds
**Symptom.** Ideas get lost while hunting for the perfect sound to play them.
**Move.** Write with the plainest timbres available (a sine, a basic piano, a dry drum kit) and no effects. An idea that survives a boring sound is a real idea; one that needs the sound to carry it is the sound's idea. Choose or design sounds afterwards, now that you know what they must do. Skip this when the genre is sound design first (textural, minimal, ambient): there the sound *is* the idea.

### Timeboxing
**Symptom.** Starting is the hard part; sessions sprawl and end badly.
**Move.** Short fixed blocks (about 20 minutes) on one kind of task, a short break, a different task. Stop when the box ends even if it is going well, so the memory of the session stays good. For an agent: bound each phase of the workflow by a deliverable, not by exhaustiveness.

### The amateur mindset
**Symptom.** Seriousness has removed the play, and with it the ideas.
**Move.** Work in a genre you do not know, where no expectation exists. Make something for its own sake. Lower stakes produce more material and better material.

### Work
**Symptom.** Waiting for flow.
**Move.** Flow arrives after effort, rarely before. Most of the process is uncomfortable; proceed anyway. When inspiration shows up, follow it as far as it goes, then return to the method.

## Progressing

### Fuzzy boundaries
**Symptom.** Sections change all at once on a bar line; the track sounds stiff and gridded.
**Move.** Make the vertical cut between two sections jagged. Coarse edits, on some tracks but never all: extend a part past the boundary; stop a part a bar or two early; cut material right before or right after the boundary so silence does the transition. Fine edits inside the parts: **anticipation**, a fill that peaks a sixteenth or an eighth before the downbeat, then silence on beat 1 and a re-entry (crash, chord) on beat 2; **hesitation**, a fill that rolls over the bar line and lands on beat 2 of the new section while the other parts have already changed. The same two gestures work on bass lines (anticipate or delay the chord change) and sustained chords (hold across the line or release early).

### Mutation over generations
**Symptom.** A great four-bar seed, and no idea what comes next.
**Move.** Duplicate the seed; make exactly one meaningful change in one parameter (a new timbre, one chord substituted, a different contour, a shifted accent, a reordered half). Duplicate the *duplicate* and change one more thing, never reverting the previous change. Eight generations give a lineage of related material, each child one step from its parent and several from the ancestor. Label each generation with the change it carries; the arrangement will choose among them.

### Asynchronous loops
**Symptom.** Loop music that stays static where the references breathe.
**Move.** Drive one instrument with several loops of different lengths at once. A 4-step pattern against a 5-step pattern yields a composite that only repeats every 20 steps; add a 3-step loop and the cycle becomes 60. The ear hears both loops and their composite. Variants: realign the loops by hand where the arrangement needs a reset; run a modulation (filter, volume) on a length unrelated to the notes; set an LFO rate that never lines up with the bar; give each loop a slightly different timbre; put one odd-length loop against otherwise square material. For a true drift, offset one loop by a fraction of a step so the loops never resynchronise.

### Linear drumming
**Symptom.** Kick keeps time, snare on 2 and 4, hats fill: correct and dull.
**Move.** Monophonic drums: no two hits on the same step, every drum equal in rank, the pattern read as a single melodic line. Groupings of 3 against the 16-step grid, resolved by a group of 2 at the bar's end, give the rolling feel; fast tempos fuse the hits into one composite line, slow tempos expose each hit. A stricter version fills every sixteenth with exactly one drum and shapes the line with velocity alone. Break the rule wherever a doubled hit sounds better.

### Silence and noise
**Symptom.** The five parameters are all there and something is still missing compared with the references.
**Move.** Decide what happens between the notes. Two poles: surgically dry, where every event enters and leaves against real silence (short sounds, fast envelopes, almost no reverb), or a constant bed of noise that is as much a part of the texture as the bass (dusty, ancient, broken). Noise sources: a silent input boosted hard, the run-out groove of a record, field recordings drowned in reverb, a feedback loop on a return track recorded and tamed. Either pole is a decision; the default of "some reverb on everything" is not.

### Melody contour
**Symptom.** Melodies wander; nothing is hummable.
**Move.** Shape the line. Balance rise against fall over the phrase. After several steps (motion by one or two semitones), leap in the opposite direction; after a leap, step back the other way, unless the line is spelling a chord. Give the phrase a single highest note, placed once, usually on a strong beat (1 or 3, or an odd subdivision), near the middle or the climax of the phrase. A good line draws an arc: it leaves home, reaches the peak once, and returns over a comparable distance.

### Hocket
**Symptom.** A melody is fine but flat; you want richness without rewriting it.
**Move.** Split the line between two or more instruments so no two play at the same time: alternate every note, every beat, or every third sixteenth (the groups will not divide the bar evenly, which is the point). Instruments with fast attacks and similar envelopes work best. Three or more voices, or one voice per note, turn a plain line into a cascade of timbres.

### The rhythm of lyrics
**Symptom.** Vocal melodies fight the words.
**Move.** Mark each syllable strong or weak as spoken, then put strong syllables on strong beats (1, 3, odd subdivisions) with longer or higher notes, weak syllables on weak beats with short notes. Breaking this is a genre decision (much rap does it on purpose), and should be made once, consciously, rather than by accident at every line.

### Parallel harmony
**Symptom.** The chord sound of deep house and its relatives will not come out of textbook voice leading.
**Move.** Copy one voicing and transpose the whole block: every note moves the same distance in the same direction (planing). The result is often non-diatonic and the key feels suspended, which is the sound. It comes historically from sampling a single chord and pitching it, and from "chord memory" keyboards; a chord generator or a MIDI tool that adds fixed intervals above the played note does the same today. Voice-led versions of the same progression sound more conventional; choose per section.

### Humanising with automation
**Symptom.** Electronic parts are correct and dead.
**Move.** Let parameters that are usually set once drift slowly over the phrase: attack and decay (which changes the perceived note length), tuning by a few cents at most, then any parameter the instrument exposes. The exact values do not matter, so randomisation often does the job of hand-drawn envelopes. Go further than filter cutoff.

### Deliberately bad listening
**Symptom.** The mix is right in the sweet spot and the track still lacks surprises.
**Move.** While composing (never while mixing), listen badly on purpose: from the corner of the room, from the next room through a door, with headphones on the desk at distance, at a whisper. Each distortion reveals phantom elements (a bass note that is not there, a rhythm between parts). Write down the ones you like, then build them for real so every listener hears them.

### Dramatic arc
**Symptom.** Sections exist, but the track is not a journey.
**Move.** Shape time as exposition (introduce the materials), rising action (vary and intensify them), climax (the densest or most intense moment: the drop, the full chorus), falling action (mirror the rise), dénouement (restate and dissolve, or fade). The arc works at every scale: a melody that rises to its peak and falls, a track, a set. Static genres (dub, minimal) build tension through sound and slow change instead, and need no climax.

## Finishing

### Arranging as a subtractive process
**Symptom.** A pool of material, and the empty timeline is paralysing.
**Move.** Fill the whole timeline on every track in seconds, without choosing: a full track's length of every pattern, duplicated. Then sculpt by removing. It is easier to hear what is wrong than to imagine what is right: a section too long announces itself; a clash tells you which part to mute. In genres whose density grows and falls, the densest section is already finished; work backwards from it, removing more the further back you go. Use insert-time and delete-time operations rather than moving blocks by hand.

### Elements of song form
**Symptom.** Verse, chorus and bridge are words you hear and cannot use.
**Move.** Verse (A): recurring, 8 to 32 bars, carries the story, builds tension; same music, different words. Chorus (B): recurring, comparable length, the hook and the title, resolution; same music and words every time. Bridge (C): once, contrasting in key, harmony or density, after at least one verse and one chorus. Pre-chorus: a lift between verse and chorus. ABABCB is most of pop radio. Instrumental electronic music usually drops these in favour of form by density: layers added and removed. Pick one model per track.

### Unique events
**Symptom.** The arrangement is complete and still sounds like loop music.
**Move.** Insert things that happen exactly once: a single sound (an odd sample, one note of an instrument never heard again), a single gesture (a one-time variation in the middle of a phrase, a transformed copy of a pattern), a single processing move (an effect chain switched on for two beats). Near a boundary they read as transition; mid-phrase and off-grid they read as interruption. Ration them: too many and the listener expects the unexpected.

### Diminishing returns
**Symptom.** The last ten percent takes forever and the mix is getting worse.
**Move.** Find the point where further changes are arbitrary rather than improvements, and stop there. Late tweaking is usually fear of committing; the first decisions were mostly right. Declare the track good enough, write down what a later version could change, and start the next one.
